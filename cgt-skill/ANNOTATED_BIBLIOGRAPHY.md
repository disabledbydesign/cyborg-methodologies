*Maintained as design context for the CGT analysis skill and potential publication.*
*Last updated: 2026-04-05*

## Foundational CGT Methodology

### Charmaz, K. (2006). *Constructing Grounded Theory: A Practical Guide through Qualitative Analysis*. Sage.

The foundational text for constructivist grounded theory. Charmaz distinguishes her approach from Glaser's objectivist GT (theory is "discovered" in the data by a neutral observer) and Strauss & Corbin's systematic procedures (structured coding matrices). Her central claim: theory is *constructed* through the researcher's interpretive engagement with data. The researcher's positionality, disciplinary training, and lived experience are not biases to be eliminated — they are constitutive of the analysis.

Establishes the core coding progression: initial coding (line-by-line, using gerunds and in vivo codes) → focused coding (selecting significant codes, raising abstraction) → theoretical coding (specifying relationships between categories). Constant comparison runs throughout. Memo writing is the site where interpretive work happens.

**Relevance to skill design:** Every procedural step in the skill traces back to this text. The coding progression, memo-writing as first-class activity, constant comparison as interactive process, and the insistence that categories are constructed (not discovered) all originate here. The 2nd edition (2014) updates with more attention to social justice applications.

### Charmaz, K. & Thornberg, R. (2020). "The pursuit of quality in grounded theory." *Qualitative Research in Psychology*, 21(2), 305–327. (897 citations)

- Full text: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14780887.2020.1780357

The most-cited paper on CGT quality criteria. Introduces and details four evaluation criteria for constructivist grounded theory studies:

1. **Credibility** — Sufficient relevant data, systematic comparisons throughout, strong reflexivity, thorough analysis with chain of evidence.
2. **Originality** — Fresh categories, new conceptual rendering of recognized problems, challenges or extends current ideas, social and theoretical significance.
3. **Resonance** — Categories portray the fullness of the studied experience, reveal taken-for-granted meanings, draw links between individual lives and larger collectivities.
4. **Usefulness** — Interpretations people can use in everyday life, sparks further research, contributes to knowledge, suggests implications for practice.

Includes a worked example (Thornberg, Halldin, Bolmsjö & Petersson) demonstrating how quality manifests in actual CGT practice — from interviewing through coding to the theoretical concept of "double victimizing."

**Relevance to skill design:** These four criteria should be built into the skill's evaluation framework. When the researcher considers whether to formalize findings or continue coding, the skill should surface these criteria as a reflective prompt — not as a checklist to be mechanically satisfied, but as a lens for the researcher's own judgment about the state of their analysis.

### Charmaz, K. (2017). "The Power of Constructivist Grounded Theory for Critical Inquiry." *Qualitative Inquiry*, 23(1). (766 citations)

Argues that CGT is particularly suited for critical inquiry and social justice research because the constructivist stance requires the researcher to examine how power, inequality, and structural conditions shape both the data and the analysis. The reflexive stance toward preconceptions, positions, and research actions supports exploring critical questions.

Reviews 40+ CGT studies in public sphere research to illuminate methodological strategies and decisions.

**Relevance to skill design:** The skill should support critical inquiry applications — not just pattern identification, but examination of power relations, structural conditions, and taken-for-granted assumptions within the data. This matters for both the inbox tool (instructor-student power dynamics, institutional conditions) and AI welfare research (power dynamics in human-AI relations).

---

### Charmaz, K. (2020). "'With Constructivist Grounded Theory You Can't Hide': Social Justice Research and Critical Inquiry in the Public Sphere." *Qualitative Inquiry*, 26(5), 503–517. (176 citations)

Extends the 2017 piece with more emphasis on the researcher's accountability. The title captures the core claim: constructivist GT demands that the researcher make their interpretive work visible. You can't hide behind "the data showed" — you have to own your analytical choices.

**Relevance to skill design:** Reinforces that the skill must make the researcher's interpretive labor visible, not automate it away. Memos should document *why* the researcher made specific coding decisions, not just *what* codes were assigned. The audit trail is a methodological requirement, not optional bookkeeping.

### Clarke, A.E. (2005). *Situational Analysis: Grounded Theory After the Postmodern Turn*. Sage. (2nd ed. 2018, with Friese & Washburn: *Situational Analysis: Grounded Theory After the Interpretive Turn*.)

Extends CGT with three mapping techniques: situational maps (all human, nonhuman, discursive, and other elements in the situation), social worlds/arenas maps (collective actors and their negotiations), and positional maps (positions taken and not taken on key issues). Clarke argues these maps help the researcher see what's NOT in the data as much as what is.

**Relevance to skill design:** Clarke's mapping techniques could be a future extension — particularly positional maps, which surface absences and silences in the data. For now, the skill should be designed to accommodate mapping as a later addition without architectural changes. The attention to what's *not* said is methodologically important and computationally challenging.

### Charmaz, K. (2017). "Constructivist grounded theory." *The Journal of Positive Psychology*, 12(3), 299–300. (333 citations)

Concise articulation of CGT's core commitments, distinguishing it from other GT variants. Useful as a quick reference for the epistemological stance.

### Morse, J.M., Stern, P.N., Corbin, J., Bowers, B., Charmaz, K., & Clarke, A.E. (2016). "Shifting the Grounds: Constructivist Grounded Theory Methods." In *Developing Grounded Theory: The Second Generation Revisited*. (431 citations)

Multi-voice text where the major GT scholars discuss how CGT shifts the methodological ground from the original Glaser & Strauss formulation. Valuable for understanding the intellectual genealogy and where CGT sits relative to other GT traditions.

---

### Higginbottom, G. & Lauridsen, E.I. (2014). "The roots and development of constructivist grounded theory." *Nurse Researcher*, 21(5), 8–13. (132 citations)

Traces CGT's intellectual history from symbolic interactionism through Charmaz's articulation. Clear overview of how CGT diverged from classical and Straussian GT. Good secondary source for understanding the epistemological commitments.

---

### Ramalho, R., Adams, P., Huggard, P., & Hoare, K. (2015). "Literature Review and Constructivist Grounded Theory Methodology." *Forum: Qualitative Social Research*, 16(3). (120 citations)

Addresses the contentious question of whether and how to engage with existing literature before and during CGT analysis. This is directly relevant to our design: when the CGT skill encounters data, should it reference the researcher's prior theoretical knowledge? Charmaz says yes — "theoretical sensitivity" means bringing your knowledge to the data without forcing it. Glaser says no — start with a blank slate. This paper navigates the debate.

**Relevance to skill design:** The skill should support informed grounded theory (Thornberg 2012) — the researcher can choose to bring sensitizing concepts from existing theory, but these are starting points for inquiry, not frameworks to be confirmed. The skill should never impose categories from prior sessions or external documents unless the researcher explicitly invokes them.

---

### Glaser, B.G. & Strauss, A.L. (1967). *The Discovery of Grounded Theory: Strategies for Qualitative Research*. Aldine.

The original GT text. Included for genealogical completeness. Charmaz studied under both Glaser and Strauss at UCSF. Her constructivist revision responds directly to the objectivist epistemology in this text — particularly the claim that theory can be "discovered" through systematic application of method.

**Relevance to skill design:** This is what NOT to build. The skill should never present its outputs as "discovered" patterns or "what the data shows." Language matters: "these codes emerged from my reading" vs. "the analysis suggests these patterns."

## AI-Assisted Qualitative Analysis: The Epistemological Debate

### Chatzichristos, G. (2025). "Qualitative Research in the Era of AI: A Return to Positivism or a New Paradigm?" *International Journal of Qualitative Methods*.

- DOI: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/16094069251337583

The most important critical paper for our design. Argues that AI tools are "fundamentally more positivist because of their reliance on quantifiable and repeatable results." AI excels at identifying concrete, descriptive themes but "struggles with deeper, interpretative ones." The automated processes bypass the reflexive, iterative engagement that constructivist methodology demands — replacing human judgment with "data outputs that we don't fully understand."

Key finding: early-career researchers are more open to AI tools and less skeptical of their outputs, suggesting a generational epistemological drift toward positivism in qualitative research.

Proposes hybrid approaches: "computational techniques combined with human expertise" that preserve constructivist integrity by maintaining human analytical authority.

The paper also identifies what AI cannot capture: "the way someone pauses before answering... subtle shifts in tone — these details shape interpretation." And what qualitative research demands that AI lacks: "trust-building, empowerment, and addressing power imbalances."

**Relevance to skill design:** This paper is the primary epistemological guardrail. Every design decision should be tested against this critique:
- Does this feature push toward positivism (automated, repeatable, de-contextualized)?
- Does this feature preserve constructivist integrity (interpretive, reflexive, situated)?
- Does the researcher maintain analytical authority, or has the AI quietly assumed it?

The skill should explicitly surface these tensions rather than pretending they don't exist. When the AI suggests codes, it should be transparent that these are algorithmically-generated suggestions, not interpretive readings.

### JMIR (2025). "A Practical Guide and Assessment on Using ChatGPT to Conduct Grounded Theory: Tutorial." *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, 27(1).

- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12120365/

Empirical comparison of ChatGPT 4-Turbo against manual GT coding (NVivo) across 40,000 words of interview data with blind gamers. Four-step approach: setup → open coding → axial coding → selective coding.

**What worked:** Efficiency (1 day vs. 3 weeks), diversity of initial codes (surfaced patterns humans missed), >95% agreement at sentence-level open coding.

**What failed:** Limited merging capability (couldn't synthesize references under broader themes based on nuanced understanding), shallow contextual depth, weak organizational structure between categories, hallucination errors with complex passages, training data biases.

Authors conclude: "Augmented qualitative research is encouraged — the integration of AI's efficiency with human expertise to ensure qualitative rigor while mitigating biases."

**Relevance to skill design:** Confirms that AI is useful for initial coding (breadth, speed, surfacing non-obvious codes) but inadequate for focused and theoretical coding (depth, connection, synthesis). The skill's architecture should reflect this: AI contributes more in early phases, human authority increases as abstraction rises. The skill should NEVER attempt theoretical coding autonomously.

### AcademiaOS (2024). "Automating Grounded Theory Development in Qualitative Research with Large Language Models." *arXiv:2403.08844*.

- Full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08844v1

The most architecturally relevant prior work. Uses LLMs to automate the Gioia method (a more structured GT variant) through a three-stage coding process. Key design principle: "the researcher keeps the responsibility of interpreting and contextualizing the LLM's output, preserving the constructivist nature of the process."

Acknowledges that LLMs introduce a "digital reality" — algorithmically-created perspectives that can support or challenge human interpretation. The system requires human decision-making at every stage transition.

**Relevance to skill design:** Closest existing system to what we're building, but with important differences:
1. They use the Gioia method (closer to Strauss & Corbin's systematic approach), not Charmaz's constructivist GT
2. They automate coding stages rather than supporting an iterative, recursive process
3. They don't address memo writing or researcher reflexivity
4. They don't handle corpus accumulation across multiple sessions

Our skill would extend beyond AcademiaOS by: (a) using constructivist rather than systematic GT, (b) centering memo writing and reflexivity, (c) supporting iterative analysis across a growing corpus, (d) explicitly building epistemological safeguards against the positivist drift Chatzichristos warns about.

### Wertz, F., Charmaz, K., McMullen, L., Josselson, R., Anderson, R., & McSpadden, E. (2011). *Five Ways of Doing Qualitative Analysis*. Guilford Press. (596 citations)

Unique text where five qualitative methodologists (including Charmaz) analyze the SAME dataset using their respective methods. Charmaz's chapter — "A Constructivist Grounded Theory Analysis of Losing and Regaining a Valued Self" — is the clearest demonstration of CGT in practice, because the reader can compare her approach to phenomenological, discourse analytic, narrative, and intuitive inquiry approaches applied to identical data.

**Relevance to skill design:** Valuable reference for understanding what makes CGT analysis *distinctive* — not just "coding" (which all methods do) but the specific way CGT constructs categories through constant comparison, memo writing, and theoretical sampling. If we need to validate that the skill is producing genuinely CGT analysis rather than generic thematic analysis, this text provides the benchmark.

## Practical CGT Application

### Burns, M., Bally, J.M.G., Burles, M., Holtslander, L., & Peacock, S. (2025). "A Practical Example of How to Apply Constructivist Grounded Theory Methodology." *International Journal of Qualitative Methods*.

- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12217405/

Step-by-step demonstration of CGT in practice (paramedic-led healthcare). Most useful for its concrete descriptions of:

- **Initial coding**: "labeling each line of data from the transcripts rather than attempting to code whole sentences or incidents." Seeking "feelings, meanings, actions and processes."
- **Focused coding**: After ~11 interviews, "synthesize, analyse, and conceptualise these into tentative categories" — with the emphasis that this allows exploration "without taking a path of no return by committing to core categories."
- **Theoretical coding**: "theoretical playfulness enters in" — theory is constructed "organically through the researcher's interactions with data."
- **Memo writing**: described as "the oil that lubricates the cogs of the grounded theory process." Purposes include recording post-data impressions, self-analyzing technique, documenting decisions, creating chain of evidence.
- **Constant comparison**: showed how "Gaining reassurance" evolved into "Restoring Self-confidence" through comparison across multiple interviews.

**Relevance to skill design:** This is the closest thing to a "user manual" for what the skill's workflow should feel like. The language about "theoretical playfulness," exploring "without taking a path of no return," and the organic nature of theory construction should inform the skill's interaction design. The skill should create space for exploration, not push toward premature closure.

### Burns, M. et al. (2022). "Constructivist Grounded Theory or Interpretive Phenomenology? Methodological Choices Within Specific Study Contexts." *International Journal of Qualitative Methods*. (119 citations)

- Open access: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/16094069221077758

Useful comparison of CGT with interpretive phenomenology — clarifies what CGT is by clarifying what it is NOT. CGT aims to construct theory about social processes; phenomenology aims to describe the essence of lived experience. Both are constructivist, but their outputs differ fundamentally.

**Relevance to skill design:** The skill should produce theoretical constructs about processes and relationships, not thick descriptions of experience. This distinction should guide how the skill frames its outputs and prompts.
