# Payoff-Setup Intro Paragraph (Post-Drafting)

**Genres:** `ea_grant`, `humanist_fellowship`, `research_paper`, `cover_letter`
**Category:** reader orientation / pipeline step
**Added:** 2026-04-17 (SFF iChange arc session)
**Updated:** 2026-04-18 (payoff-setup distinction added — SFF + Harvard Divinity refinement)

## The Move

After drafting the long-form application or cover letter, add a brief introductory paragraph (~80–150 words) at the very top of the document, BEFORE Section 1 or the main body. Its job is **not to summarize the document**. Its job is to **set up a specific payoff the rest of the document delivers.**

## The Critical Distinction — Payoff-Setup vs. Generic Summary

A generic summary tells the reader *what the document is about*: "This grant funds X to do Y through Z." This wastes the most valuable real estate in the application. Generic intros could describe any project in the same general area.

A payoff-setup intro tells the reader *what specific promise the document is about to deliver*: a claim, finding, or distinctive feature that only this work has. It creates forward-leaning expectation.

**The generic-swap test:** Read your intro. Could it describe any scholar or project in the same general area? If yes, it is generic — rewrite. If no — if the specific payoff would not apply to most other applications in the pool — the intro is doing its job.

## Content Checklist

The intro should still cover:
- Who the applicant is (explicitly or implicitly)
- What the grant/position would support
- What gets built, studied, delivered, or contributed
- The key mechanism, finding, or claim the work rests on (by name, if it has one)
- Deployment infrastructure or institutional fit (where relevant)
- Total ask + duration (for grants) or position + fit (for jobs)

But these are boxes to cover *within* a payoff-setup structure — not the structure itself.

## Payoff-Setup Patterns

Effective payoff-setup intros typically follow one of these shapes:

**Pattern A (problem → alternative):**
> "[Problem in domain] through [specific mechanism / architectural feature]. This grant funds [deployment / study] of an alternative — one [distinctive qualifier: already built / demonstrated empirically / with real users / at particular scale]..."

**Pattern B (finding in flight):**
> "[Specific finding or event, stated plainly] that [makes the claim novel / surprising / consequential]. [Brief account of how it arose.] [What the rest of the document shows/argues.]"

**Pattern C (methodological move operationalized):**
> "[Single methodological move, named plainly]. [What it produces that other approaches cannot.] [Genealogy or application visible in the body that follows.]"

Common property: each names something specific that makes the rest of the document worth reading carefully rather than skimming.

## When to Use

- Any grant or fellowship application with a long-form narrative attachment
- Any cover letter for positions valuing intellectual originality or distinctive contribution
- Whenever a skimming reader would have to wait past the first several paragraphs to understand what's distinctive about the work
- When genre convention favors executive-summary-style orientation (most grants do)

## When NOT to Use

- Short applications where a payoff-setup takes up disproportionate space (under ~1,500 words total)
- When the application form has a separate abstract or project-summary short-form field already serving this function
- When the application's key argument is genuinely modest (a standard renewal, a routine request) and claiming a dramatic payoff would read as inflation

## Why After Drafting, Not Before

The intro is a promise the document has to deliver on. You can't write the promise well until you know what the document actually argues — what the specific claim turned out to be, what the concrete evidence is, what the distinctive structural features are. Pre-drafting intros produce placeholders that go stale as the draft develops (terms drift, emphasis shifts, the argument lands somewhere different from the outline) and usually read as generic.

This is how many human writers naturally draft — introduction written last. This move makes that explicit and adds it as a pipeline step.

## Pipeline Position

This move belongs AFTER:
- Story pass (Phase 2)
- Section reorganization (Phase 3)
- Voice-check contamination pass (Phase 4)
- Anti-generic pass
- Gold-standard language review

And BEFORE:
- Sequential workshop with the user (Phase 5)
- Reviewer-agent pass

## Example 1 — SFF iChange Application (April 2026)

**Generic (first draft, replaced):**

> "This grant funds iChange Collaborative to deploy and study two open-source tools that redesign how AI evaluates student work. Autograder4Canvas (MIT license) is a classroom insights tool… Reframe (GPL v3.0) is a critical-theory framework engine…"

Describes the project. Creates no specific expectation. Could be rewritten with different tool names and describe many other AI-in-education grant applications.

**Payoff-setup (after rewrite):**

> "AI evaluatory tools in K-12 and higher education are producing demographic bias through a specific architectural mechanism. This grant funds the deployment and study of an alternative — one this PI has already built, demonstrated empirically, and used in real classrooms. Autograder4Canvas (MIT license) replaces the binary 'concern / no-concern' classifiers common in AI student-welfare systems with generative observation: the AI describes what a student's work shows rather than sorting it..."

The payoff is explicit: *tools are built, finding is demonstrated, we're proposing to scale*. The reader now expects to see the finding, the tools, the scaling plan — and the body delivers each.

## Example 2 — Harvard Divinity Cover Letter (April 2026)

**Generic (first draft, replaced):**

> "Hakope, the heles-hayv of Pvlvcekolv, asked me whether I had considered that the figure wasn't a bird. I had been presenting a queer-theory reading of gendered bird imagery on Mississippian objects… The framework I had brought to the evidence, not the evidence itself, had produced the question I thought I was answering. I have worked with that observation across every project since..."

Strong story. But as an opening for this specific position, it sets up a general methodological principle ("I interrogate frameworks") rather than a specific payoff the letter delivers. Another anthropologist with a similar commitment could write the same opening.

**Payoff-setup (after rewrite):**

> "This March, an argument emerged in a conversation between me and five Claude instances that neither of us could have produced alone. I brought a critique and an architecture... What emerged in the relational field was a formulation neither side brought: the AI welfare field's indicator-based assessment apparatus is not epistemically limited. It is ontologically malformed. It searches for a property in an entity when what it should attend to is a relation in a field."

The payoff is specific: a novel finding (AI welfare assessment is ontologically malformed) produced through a novel collaboration (human + five AI instances producing together what neither could alone). The reader expects to see this finding developed, the collaboration explained, and the fit to Harvard Divinity's cross-Harvard structure articulated — and the letter delivers each of those.

The Hakope story still appears in the letter body where it illustrates the methodological move. The opening reserves that real estate for what makes *this* application distinctive to *this* position.

## Diagnostic Questions

Before finalizing the intro, test:

1. **Generic-swap test:** Could this describe any application in the same pool? If yes, rewrite.
2. **Promise test:** Does the paragraph make a specific promise the body delivers on? If no, rewrite.
3. **Skim test:** If a reviewer read only this paragraph, would they understand what's distinctive about this application? Or would they understand "someone applied for something standard"?
4. **Naming test:** Does any term used in the intro (a named mechanism, a specific finding, a distinctive methodological move) appear later in the document delivering on the promise? If a named term appears only in the intro, it's not actually doing work — it's decoration.

## Why It Works

- **Respects reviewer time** — skim-friendly without being skim-defeat
- **Creates cognitive orientation** — a specific promise establishes expectation; the body delivers on it
- **Resolves the distinctiveness question** — "why should we read this?" gets answered in the first paragraph, which means later sections can make arguments rather than also bear the burden of establishing why they matter
- **Frees the body** to make its argument without also carrying orientation load
- **Earns the rest of the document** — the body serves a promise, not a wandering mandate
- **Signals genre fluency** — reviewers see the applicant understands what a strong opening looks like, which creates good-faith reading posture for the rest
